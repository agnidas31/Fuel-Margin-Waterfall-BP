From One Margin Number to Every Cent Explained

Fuel margin is reported in cents per gallon, and one cent across hundreds of millions of gallons is millions of dollars. But a single number hides everything behind it. The Fuel Margin Waterfall broke it into the drivers that move it, so pricing and supply teams could see not just what the margin was, but why.

The project

A margin waterfall for the US fuel network

Every margin conversation used to start with “let's go and find out why.” I owned the data behind the report end to end: bringing pricing, supply cost, invoice and volume data together, making every component add up, and building the Tableau waterfalls.

The starting point: a number with no story

When margin dropped, the business couldn't quickly tell whether the market had moved, the brand premium had changed, supply terms had worsened, or costs had shifted. Each has a different owner and a different fix.

Without the breakdown, a one-cent drop was a $5 million mystery.

Why it was hard: every driver lived somewhere else

This wasn't a charting problem. Every piece of the margin came from a different system, at a different level of detail.

None of these lined up naturally, and a waterfall only works if every bar adds up exactly to the total.

The method: one grain, volume-weighted

I aligned every input to site × day × grade, and stored each component in dollars rather than cents per gallon. Conversion happens only at the very end, so the parts always sum to the whole.

A simple average gives a small site the same weight as a large one. Volume-weighting keeps every bar honest, and each refresh is checked against the finance margin.

Reading the waterfall

Standard margin starts from what anyone could earn at the market's lowest rack price, then adds and subtracts each driver.

At 500 million gallons, 8.5 cents per gallon is $42.5 million of standard margin, and every cent of it is attributed to a driver.

What moved this month?

When margin fell by a cent, the waterfall showed which bar moved.

Brand value held, so pricing wasn't the problem. The bp premium widened, so the conversation goes to supply, with the size of the gap already on the table.

Three layers of margin

The same approach extends beyond the fuel itself, to what bp's supply chain adds and to environmental and regulatory effects.

One view, three layers, and the same breakdown from the whole network down to a single site and grade.

Why this mattered

Changing the question

The waterfall moved every margin conversation from “what was our margin?” to “what moved our margin, and who owns it?” Root causes that used to take an investigation were read straight off the chart, twice as fast, and supply decisions got sharper because the gap was already quantified.
